What does research-informed mean for this app?

First Volo Morphology is a research-informed tool for teaching and practicing morphology. Educators can use it alongside core literacy instruction and in targeted intervention.

  • Morphological awareness. Notice, break apart, and combine the meaningful parts in words.
  • Word-part knowledge. Learn what specific prefixes, roots, and suffixes mean or do.
  • Word solving. Use word parts and context to figure out unfamiliar words.

Studies of morphology instruction support practices used in First Volo, including explicit teaching of meaningful word parts, comparing related words, and applying a taught part to a new word. The research linked here evaluates those practices and other interventions. First Volo itself has yet to be evaluated in a controlled effectiveness study. Its Pre/Post forms provide informal information for planning instruction. What the Pre/Post can show.

Why teach morphology?

Meaningful word parts help learners connect what they hear, what they see in print, what a word means, and how it works in a sentence. For example, -ed marks past time in jumped, played, and painted. Its spelling stays consistent across three pronunciations. Apel & Werfel (2014) · Green & Wolter (2025).

Why are the Routes sequenced this way?

Route 1 starts with familiar bases and common affixes, such as tell → retell. Route 2 introduces more Greek and Latin root families, such as portable and transport. Route 3 adds more layered or less transparent families, such as propel and compel. The progression increases the complexity of the words and the reasoning they require. Frequency helps us decide what to include. Transparency, usefulness, and grade standards help us decide where it fits. The three Routes are First Volo’s curriculum design. White, Sowell, & Yanagihara (1989) · Apel, Henbest, & Petscher (2023) · Scope and Sequence.

Grade standards. The Massachusetts ELA/Literacy Framework and Common Core connect familiar affixes and bases to meaning in grades 2–3 (L.2.4b–c and L.3.4b–c), Greek and Latin word parts in grades 4–5 (L.4.4b and L.5.4b), and checking an inferred meaning in grades 6–8 (L.6.4d, L.7.4d, and L.8.4d). Use the learner’s grade for standards and their current work to choose a Route. Massachusetts ELA/Literacy Framework · Common Core Language Standards.

How are lessons and activities organized?

Within a Route, meaning groups help educators compare related parts or word families. Choose a starting part based on the learner’s needs. Within a lesson, model a clear example, compare or sort related words, then try the part in a fresh word. Use Break It Apart to see how a learner analyzes a word and Change It to see whether they can choose a form that fits a sentence. Route 3 adds a sentence check in Figure It Out. Manyak et al. (2018) · Collins (2023) · Gellert et al. (2021).

Teaching guidance. Massachusetts DESE’s Mass Literacy resource connects explicit word-part teaching with spelling and classroom vocabulary. The What Works Clearinghouse guide puts analysis of word parts within a broader reading intervention. Mass Literacy · Vocabulary and Morphology · What Works Clearinghouse · Grades 4–9 reading interventions.

Practitioner example. Joan Sedita shows how teachers can model looking inside an unfamiliar word for meaningful parts and around it for context clues. Keys to Literacy · Using Morphology to Teach Vocabulary.

Who might benefit from this practice?

Educators can use it in classroom word study or for targeted support. Studies have included elementary learners, students with decoding or spelling difficulties, and adolescents who are multilingual or have language disorder. Those groups were studied with different teaching approaches and outcomes. Choose a starting point from the learner’s current work and watch how they respond. Park et al. (2020) · Crosson et al. (2025) · Glisson et al. (2026).

What improvements have studies found?

Studies show gains in word-part knowledge, word reading, and spelling, especially with taught material. Some studies also found gains in spelling untaught words and using related forms in sentences. Evidence for broad comprehension and essay-writing gains is less consistent. These findings describe the interventions that were studied. To see how a learner carries the work into class, check a fresh word, a reading sample, or an original sentence. Colenbrander et al. (2024) · McCutchen et al. (2022) · More on learner profiles and outcomes.

How morphology supports reading, spelling, and writing

Morphological awareness helps learners notice how meaningful word parts connect a word’s pronunciation, spelling, meaning, and job in a sentence. For example, -ed marks past time and keeps the same spelling in jumped, played, and painted, even though it sounds different in each word.

Who has been studied?

  • Elementary learners. A review of 28 studies in English-speaking countries covered primary-school students. Colenbrander et al. (2024).
  • Learners with literacy difficulties. Studies included children with reading, spelling, or language difficulties. In a small randomized study of 16 children with spelling difficulties, instruction that combined morphology with other language skills improved reading and spelling accuracy. Goodwin & Ahn (2010) · Kirk & Gillon (2009).
  • Multilingual adolescents and adolescents with developmental language disorder. Studies with multilingual adolescents found gains in academic word knowledge and word-part analysis. A small trial with adolescents with language disorder found that learners could apply taught affixes to new roots. Crosson et al. (2025) · Glisson et al. (2026).

What outcomes have been demonstrated?

Word reading and spelling improved in the elementary review, especially for taught words. The review found gains in spelling untaught words. Results for reading untaught words and for reading comprehension were less clear. An earlier review of learners with literacy difficulties also found gains in phonological awareness, morphology, and vocabulary. Colenbrander et al. (2024) · Goodwin & Ahn (2010).

  • Word forms in sentences. Fourth and fifth graders receiving morphology-rich vocabulary instruction got better at changing taught and untaught words to fit sentences. Standardized spelling gains were clearer than gains on the essay measure. McCutchen et al. (2022).
  • Word analysis in dyslexia. A small study in grades 4–6 found gains on measures designed to capture morphological analysis, decoding, and spelling. Results on broader standardized measures were inconclusive. Mendes & Kirby (2024).
  • Reading comprehension. In grades 5–8, grammatical and sound–spelling knowledge about word parts were linked with comprehension in students with limited reading vocabulary. This study measured associations between skills. Goodwin, Petscher, & Tock (2020).

Connect word study to reading and writing

  • Massachusetts DESE · Mass Literacy teaching guidance. Teach word meanings, word parts, and spelling explicitly. Show written words as students say them and discuss what they mean. Help learners connect new words with words they already know and use them in meaningful contexts. Vocabulary and Morphology.
  • Apel & Werfel (2014) · Tutorial and research overview. Knowing how written words preserve meaningful parts can help students decode and spell related words. The authors describe ways to connect word-part instruction to reading and spelling. Using Morphological Awareness Instruction to Improve Written Language Skills.
  • Green & Wolter (2025) · Clinical focus article and case example. The authors show how to teach pronunciation, spelling, meaning, and grammar together in an academic unit for learners with language and literacy difficulties. The approach connects word study to reading comprehension and written expression. Morphological Awareness and Academic Literacy.

To check these broader skills, collect a reading, spelling, or writing sample alongside word-part practice. Classroom and intervention examples.

What assessment studies suggest

Nelson-Strouts and Bridges (2024) suggest that generating a word form, breaking a word apart, and judging a word relationship can reveal different aspects of morphological awareness. Their tutorial also explains how spoken and written tasks place different demands on reading, spelling, and responding.   • Spoken and written responses. Henbest and Apel (2026) found that students in grades 3–6 performed better on spoken word-form tasks. Written performance still provided additional information about word reading and spelling. The two formats used different words and sentences. Comparing spoken and written word-form tasks.
  • Cues and sentence context. Wolter, Gibson, and Slocum (2020) found preliminary support for a first-grade measure that offered progressively more help. In a separate study of third and fifth graders, Ram and colleagues (2013) found that the apparent benefit of sentence context was less clear after accounting for how often students read. What Does Research Support?

Morphological instruction explicitly teaches students to notice and use meaningful word parts such as prefixes, roots, bases, and suffixes.

META-ANALYSIS

Goodwin & Ahn, 2013

This meta-analysis synthesized 30 independent studies of morphological instruction with school-age children.

The authors reported a moderate overall positive effect and significant effects for morphological knowledge, phonological awareness, vocabulary, decoding, and spelling.

Overall: d = .32 Morphology: d = .44 Vocabulary: d = .34 Decoding: d = .59 Spelling: d = .30

This meta-analysis did not find significant effects for reading comprehension or fluency. First Volo therefore does not claim that morphology practice alone directly produces gains in every area of reading.

View study ↗
SYSTEMATIC REVIEW

Bowers, Kirby, & Deacon, 2010

This review examined 22 morphological-intervention studies involving students from preschool through Grade 8.

The review found benefits from morphological instruction across literacy outcomes and concluded that it was particularly useful when integrated with other aspects of literacy instruction.

View study ↗
GRADES 5–6

Goodwin, 2016

In a study of 203 fifth- and sixth-grade students, morphological problem-solving was integrated with comprehension-strategy instruction.

Students receiving the morphology component showed stronger outcomes on measures of vocabulary and generation of morphologically related words.

Significant differences were not found for every literacy measure, including reading comprehension and word-reading fluency.

View study ↗
ADOLESCENT ENGLISH LEARNERS

Crosson, McKeown, Moore, & Ye, 2019

This study examined vocabulary instruction with and without explicit analysis of bound Latin roots among 84 English Learner adolescents.

Instruction that included Latin roots produced stronger morphological problem-solving of unfamiliar words, supporting the use of roots as tools for analyzing new academic vocabulary.

Learning of the directly taught academic word meanings was similar across the two instructional conditions, illustrating why morphology is one component of vocabulary instruction rather than a replacement for rich word teaching.

View study ↗
What First Volo takes from this research

First Volo emphasizes explicit teaching of meaningful word parts, repeated analysis across word families, and opportunities to use morphology as a problem-solving strategy. These lessons can support phonics, vocabulary instruction, and reading in context as part of comprehensive literacy instruction.

Applying Morphology in Context

In Use It, students consider a word’s parts and the sentence when deciding which word fits. In Change It, they choose a word form that fits the sentence’s grammar. In Route 3 Figure It Out, students use a sentence clue to check an inferred meaning and decide whether to keep or revise it.

This design reflects the complementary use of context clues and morphological analysis represented in the Common Core vocabulary standards.

Important distinction

The Common Core standards identify skills students should develop. They do not provide a single official grade-by-grade list assigning every individual prefix, root, suffix, or vocabulary word to a grade.

First Volo Migration Routes are therefore instructional recommendations. They are informed by standards expectations together with morphological complexity, transparency, word accessibility, and curricular sequencing.

How Words and Word Parts Are Selected

Different resources answer different questions. First Volo uses them as complementary evidence rather than treating any one standard, vocabulary list, assessment, or curriculum resource as a complete word-to-grade placement system.

Wordsmyth Vocabulary Inventory (WVI)

The WVI contains 10,000 important vocabulary words organized into five approximate difficulty bands. First Volo uses WVI information as one indicator of vocabulary familiarity and accessibility.

New General Service List (NGSL)

NGSL 1.2 is a corpus-based list of high-utility general English vocabulary. First Volo uses NGSL matches and frequency information as evidence about broad lexical frequency — not as a morphology grade-placement system.

NGSL Project ↗

New Academic Word List (NAWL)

NAWL 1.2 is designed to identify important general academic English vocabulary and to work alongside NGSL 1.2. First Volo uses NAWL matches as one indicator that a word occurs in academic language.

NAWL Project ↗

Academic Word Finder (Achieve the Core)

The Academic Word Finder helps educators identify high-value Tier 2 vocabulary in text. Its own guidance emphasizes that the tool supports rather than replaces teacher judgment.

First Volo uses it as an additional lens for considering the academic usefulness of vocabulary.

Academic Word Finder ↗

MAP Growth (NWEA)

First Volo uses MAP Growth resources as evidence of instructional relevance and relative difficulty when vocabulary, concepts, or language skills appear within NWEA's assessment framework.

RIT-to-Concept resources and the Learning Continuum provide information about concepts and skills represented within RIT ranges and can help educators interpret the relative difficulty of assessed content. Learning Continuum statements describe content represented in MAP Growth item pools; they do not indicate which items an individual student actually encountered.

The RIT scale itself is grade-independent. NWEA also provides grade-level norms and comparative data that can help place RIT performance in grade context. First Volo therefore considers NWEA evidence alongside other sources rather than treating an individual RIT band as a fixed word-to-grade assignment.

Curriculum Corroboration

Published instructional resources can provide additional evidence that particular morphemes or word families are used in instruction at particular grade levels. First Volo uses resources such as Lexia morphology materials, EL Education's Grades 6–8 affix resources, and grade-specific roots-and-affixes collections as curriculum corroboration.

These sources provide evidence of instructional use. They are not treated as normative lexical grade assignments and do not independently determine a First Volo introduction or practice band.

First Volo Instructional Analysis

External sources are combined with direct analysis of the word itself: its morphemes, segmentation, transparency, structural complexity, whole-word accessibility, academic usefulness, and suitability for a particular activity.

This instructional analysis is a First Volo design decision, not a classification supplied by any external organization.

4

First Volo's Multi-Dimensional Approach

Word difficulty is not treated as a single variable.

Morpheme Introduction

When First Volo recommends introducing a particular prefix, root, combining form, or suffix.

Morphology Practice Band

When analysis of a particular word and its morphological structure is instructionally appropriate.

Word Accessibility

An estimate of when the whole word itself is reasonably accessible to learners.

Vocabulary Level

Standard Words combines Familiar and Academic vocabulary. Educators can add Stretch Words. Word familiarity is considered separately from the complexity of its word parts.

Transparency

How clearly the visible word parts and their meanings help a learner understand the whole word.

Activity Eligibility

Whether a word is well suited for recognition, decomposition, inference, or independent word building.

Why separate these dimensions?

A familiar word can have opaque internal morphology, while an unfamiliar academic word can contain highly transparent word parts. Separating these dimensions allows First Volo to control both the language demands and the morphological demands of an activity.
