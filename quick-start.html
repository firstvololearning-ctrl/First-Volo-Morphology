What students will learn

Students learn to recognize prefixes, roots, and suffixes, explain what each part adds to a word, and use those clues to figure out unfamiliar words. They also build related words and choose forms that fit a sentence.

Compare end-of-route outcomes

By the end of each Migration Route, students will be able to do the following.

  • Route 1 · Typically grades 2–3

    Identify familiar prefixes and suffixes, explain how they change a base word’s meaning or form, and use them to figure out, build, and use related words. For example, use what re- adds to retell to work out repack.

  • Route 2 · Typically grades 4–5

    Break words into meaningful parts, including Greek and Latin roots. Use those parts to explain how words are related, figure out unfamiliar words, and build and use related forms. For example, use port (“carry”) to connect transport and portable.

  • Route 3 · Typically grades 6–8

    Analyze and build words with roots and affixes, and choose related forms that fit a sentence. Infer unfamiliar meanings even when the connection between a part and the whole word is indirect. In Figure It Out, choose a likely meaning, then use a sentence clue to keep or revise it. For example, connect pel (“push” or “drive”) to compel, then use the sentence to tell what someone was made to do.

What learners practice in each format

All formats draw on the same Scope and Sequence. Learners encounter the word parts and activities you select. Use the Route to choose the complexity, then choose the response you want to hear or see.

  • Independent online only. In Learn, learners meet a part, see examples, and can sort words and choose or say a shared meaning. Practice activities let them identify parts, break words apart, infer meanings, build words, and select words or forms in sentences. In Route 3 Figure It Out, learners choose a meaning, then use the sentence to keep or revise it. The app records on-screen responses. An explanation spoken aloud needs someone to listen if you want to assess it.
  • Teacher-guided only. Choose a part and a teaching task. Model a new skill or start with an independent attempt on a taught skill. Listen as the learner explains, builds, or produces a related form. With a task such as teacher-guided Use It, ask for an original sentence and observe whether the learner needs a cue. Where available, try a fresh application item before Check Transfer.
  • Teacher-guided and independent. Teach or reteach a part together, assign a matching activity for independent practice, then use the saved responses and a fresh example to plan what to teach next. A learner can also practice independently first so you can respond to the errors you see.
  • Add printables to either or both. Choose a worksheet or packet for the parts already taught. Learners can move cards, build and record words, sort the same words two ways, explain their reasoning, or say and write sentences with the teacher. Use the catalog’s timing notes to choose a resource. The teacher can record observations from paper work.

What to include over time. Teach what a part means or does. Have the learner compare related words and explain the part’s contribution. Give them a chance to figure out a new word and to build or produce a form that fits a sentence. Revisit the part later and check how independently they can use it in a fresh word or context. If the goal includes spoken or written use, collect an original response you can listen to or read.

In a lesson. When a word part is new, model it in a familiar word, sort and explain related words when the comparison fits, then try the part in a new word. Route 3 adds a sentence check in Figure It Out before the learner keeps or revises their answer.

Sort, explain, and sort again

Use this teacher-guided activity once learners have studied the parts they will sort. Each sample lists the sets those parts come from. Give them words that can be grouped in more than one meaningful way. Ask them to name their groups, explain their choices, and then find a different connection.

For example, rewrite and replay share re-. On a second sort, rewrite can go with writing because both share the base write. Ask what the learner noticed when they moved the word, then have them use a word in a sentence.

How the skills build


  1. Know the parts

    Learn what meaningful word parts mean or do.


  2. Notice how words work

    Find patterns and explain what related words have in common.


  3. Reason with the parts

    Break apart or build words and explain what each part contributes.


  4. Put the parts to work

    Use word parts to choose a meaning or form that fits a sentence.


  5. Use it somewhere new

    Apply the strategy to an unfamiliar word or a new context.

These stages describe increasingly independent work. Start where the learner needs practice and return to Learn whenever they need an example or a refresher.

How to check progress

  • Scored online practice. Saved responses are tracked automatically when a student works independently or when you select the learner and complete a scored activity together in Work with a learner. Open Learners & Progress to review the results.
  • Teacher-led or paper practice. Observe how the learner explains a taught part and uses it in a fresh word. Check Transfer in Session Materials gives you a quick way to see whether the learner recognizes the part and can use it to work out the word’s meaning. To count eligible practice toward a set token, use Record an observed response in Learners & Progress. Follow the teacher-led set routine to plan a later check.
  • At the end of a route. After earning all the route’s tokens, students can open the Migration Challenge from My Migration Map. It checks how independently they apply familiar parts to unfamiliar words.

How teacher-led support works

When checking a skill the learner has already been taught, begin with an independent attempt. For a new skill, model it first.

  • Try independently. See what the learner can do before adding a cue.
  • Use the least support needed. Match the prompt to the difficulty, such as finding the part, reading the word, or explaining the meaning.
  • Retry the same task. After support, ask the learner to try the same skill again and reduce help as they’re ready.
  • Try a fresh example. Check whether the learner can use the skill with a new word or in a new sentence.

Example teaching goals and standards

Spoken language goal. Use regular past tense to tell what happened.

  1. Model. In Learn, say “Yesterday we walked to the park.” Ask what -ed tells us about when the action happened.
  2. Try a new verb aloud. Show a picture of someone kicking a ball. Say “Use kick to tell me what she did yesterday.” Listen for a response such as “She kicked the ball.”
  3. Note the response. Record whether the learner used the past-tense form on their own or after a cue. If you give a cue, have the learner try the same sentence again.

What the Migration Map tracks

My Migration Map is the starting point in Student Mode. Learners can start their practice, view any route, and play at unlocked game stops. Changing the map view keeps the learner’s current practice choices.

Anchor Words. Revisit an unlocked anchor word below the map to help remember what was taught. Each PDF card shows how the parts help explain the whole word. Students unlock cards by earning the needed set tokens and reaching the matching stops. Shared online activities completed through Work with a learner can count when the learner is selected and the activity saves eligible responses. A learner can complete one set independently and the next with a teacher, keeping the same map progress. After a guided or paper lesson, select the learner in Learners & Progress and open Record an observed response. Record what the learner did on the first attempt and check again on a later date. Eligible responses count toward the same word-part knowledge and application requirements as scored online practice. Mark responses that needed a cue so you can plan follow-up practice.

Review an anchor word. Say or show the word with the explanation covered. Ask the learner to recall one taught part and its meaning, then explain how it helps in the whole word. Open the card to check. Try the same part in a different word the next time you meet. Print an optional Anchor Word review sheet.

Review earlier sets. On Route 2, students can choose a Route 1 set. On Route 3, they can choose a set from Route 1 or 2. Check this set asks one question per part in a familiar example. Students can open Learn for any part they miss or go to today’s lesson when ready. The check gives immediate feedback and saves responses as review practice.

Assign a set review. In Set up independent learning, choose the learner’s current Route and a Set review. Save it on its own or alongside a lesson. It appears as Check assigned set in Student Mode. When no set review is assigned, Route 2 and 3 Learn lessons may open with up to two questions on earlier word parts. The warm-up gives priority to parts last practiced at least a week ago. For learners with little saved practice, it can check a few common earlier parts. Students can skip to the lesson.

Each route has learning sets that group word parts for progress tracking. Route 1 has four, Route 2 has seven, and Route 3 has five. The app looks at saved responses across sessions, including which parts learners recognize and how they apply them in activities such as Break It Apart or Build Words. Each earned set token moves Volo one stop. After earning all the route’s tokens, learners can try the Migration Challenge to reach Winter Home.

Game markers show which sets unlock games. Game scores stay in the game. Educators can preview games from Educator tools and use the Scope and Sequence to choose what to teach.

Why First Volo is designed this way

Why there are three Routes
  • Route 1 · Typically grades 2–3. Start with familiar bases, prefixes, and endings, such as play → played and tell → retell. Apel & Diehm (2014), early elementary intervention.
  • Route 2 · Typically grades 4–5. Continue with affixes and introduce more Greek and Latin roots, such as port (“carry”) in portable and transport. Manyak et al. (2018), teaching roots and affixes.
  • Route 3 · Typically grades 6–8. Work with more complex word families, such as pel (“push” or “drive”) in propel and compel. Crosson et al. (2019), Latin roots with adolescent English learners.

Use the learner’s responses to choose a starting Route. Learners in the same grade can show different strengths across word-part tasks. Apel, Henbest, & Petscher (2022).

Use the learner’s actual grade to look up standards. Grades 2–3 use familiar affixes and bases to work out meaning (L.2.4b–c and L.3.4b–c). Grades 4–5 use Greek and Latin roots and affixes as clues (L.4.4b and L.5.4b). Grades 6–8 also verify inferred meanings (L.6.4d, L.7.4d, and L.8.4d). Massachusetts ELA/Literacy Framework · Common Core Language Standards.

How we choose word parts and example words
  • Which parts we teach. Frequency helps us prioritize useful parts such as un-, re-, -s/-es, -ed, and -ing. We group parts by shared meaning or grammatical function when comparison helps students see a pattern. White, Sowell, & Yanagihara (1989) · Manyak, Baumann, & Manyak (2018).
  • Which words we teach first. Start with a familiar word whose parts are easy to explain, such as retell. Later, use examples such as compel, where connecting the root to the whole word takes more reasoning. A familiar word can teach a later part. For example, performance makes -ance easier to explain in Route 3. Apel et al. on base transparency.
  • What we checked. We compared our word inventory with general frequency lists (GSL, NGSL, and BNC/COCA), academic vocabulary lists (AWL, NAWL, AVL, and AKL), and school subject lists (SVL and MSVL). For selected words, we checked modern meanings and Wordsmyth Vocabulary Inventory levels, and consulted Etymonline to clarify historical word-family relationships. Wordsmyth Vocabulary Inventory · Etymonline entry for performance.
  • How we use the results. Frequency helps us choose words students may encounter again. Subject lists help us choose examples for classroom reading and discussion. A recorded WVI level estimates whole-word difficulty, and we keep the evidence for a base word separate from evidence for its related forms. Grade standards guide the skill expectations. Word-part complexity, clarity of the example, and learner responses guide where to start.
Why lessons model, sort, and try a new word
  1. Model. Show what the part contributes in a familiar word. Manyak et al. (2018).
  2. Sort and explain. Ask what the words have in common or what job the ending does. Manyak et al. (2018) · Goodwin (2016).
  3. Try a new word. Check whether the learner can apply the part to an unfamiliar example. Gellert et al. (2021).
  4. Check the sentence in Route 3. Have the learner use a sentence clue to keep or revise their inferred meaning. Graves et al. (2021) · Baumann et al. (2003).
